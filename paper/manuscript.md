## Abstract

*(≤250 words; must avoid "new", "novel", "first", "unprecedented" and similar)*

Journals are beginning to consider language models for manuscript handling, but
submitted manuscripts are unpublished, so the models must run on hardware the editorial
office controls. This makes the capability of locally hosted models — rather than of
hosted services — the relevant question, and that capability has not been measured on
editorial work. We built a benchmark of eight editorial tasks from a journal's actual
Instructions for Authors, five manuscripts carrying 63 verified seeded defects, and the
three published reviews of a real preprint. We evaluated 21 models spanning 3.3 to
81 GB across 283 measured conditions on a bench-top workstation with unified memory,
a machine at a price and form factor an individual laboratory or editorial office can
adopt. Guideline compliance is reached at a useful level: the strongest single model
detects 34 of 40 seeded violations with no false positives, and it occupies 17 GB.
Model size does not order quality on any task, with rank correlations between weight
size and score lying from −0.27 to +0.25; within one model family the 65 GB member
scores below its 13 GB sibling. A deterministic checker of regular expressions and
arithmetic detects 30 of the same 40 violations in under 0.1 s with no model at all,
and combining it with the 17 GB model reaches 39 of 40, so the marginal contribution of
the language model is nine items. Peer-review support recovers 7 of 12 reference points
and does not substitute for a reviewer. Prompt structure moved scores further than any
property of the hardware.

**Key words:** local language models, editorial workflow, benchmark, peer review,
research infrastructure

## ◀ Significance ▶

*(≤100 words; also published on the Biophysical Society of Japan website)*

Manuscripts under review are confidential, so any language model a journal uses must run
on its own hardware. How capable such locally hosted models are at editorial work was
untested. Measuring eight editorial tasks on a desk-side workstation, we find that a
17 GB model detects 34 of 40 seeded guideline violations without false alarms, that
model size predicts quality on none of the tasks, and that three quarters of those
violations are found by a short program with no model at all. Editorial support of this
kind is within reach of ordinary laboratory hardware.

## Introduction

Language models now participate in most stages of research, and their arrival at the
publication stage has been rapid enough that journals are formulating policy while the
tools are still changing [1,2]. Assessments of what these systems accelerate span
hypothesis generation, experimental execution, interpretation, and manuscript
preparation [3,4,5]. The editorial office sits at the end of this pipeline and receives
its output: as upstream stages accelerate, submission volume rises, and the reviewer
pool does not grow to match [4].

Two editorial activities are natural candidates for support by a language model.
Checking a manuscript against submission guidelines is rule-governed and repetitive.
Supporting peer review — surfacing points a reviewer might otherwise miss — is not
rule-governed but is bounded. Automated editorial handling is already operating in
practice: the Journal of Open Source Software conducts its editorial workflow through
bots acting on repository issues, which shows that the surrounding machinery is not the
obstacle.

One constraint shapes what can actually be deployed. **Submitted manuscripts are
unpublished and confidential.** Sending them to a hosted model service is incompatible
with the confidentiality a journal owes its authors, so the model must run on hardware
the editorial office controls. The relevant question is therefore not how capable
frontier hosted models are, but how capable *locally hosted* models are on this work —
and that has not been measured. Existing benchmarks for publication-stage tasks
[6,7,8] evaluate models against each other on hosted infrastructure; published
local-inference results report throughput on standard models rather than quality on
editorial tasks.

We therefore constructed the editorial work itself as a benchmark and measured open-weight
models on it. Eight tasks were built from a journal's actual Instructions for Authors,
from manuscripts carrying defects we seeded and verified, and from the published reviews
of a real preprint (Figures 1 and 2).

A second question follows immediately from the first: what does it take to run these
models. We answered it on a single NVIDIA DGX Spark. The choice is deliberate. This is a
desk-side machine with 119 GiB of unified memory at a price and form factor an individual
laboratory, or a small editorial office, can adopt without institutional procurement —
so a capability demonstrated here is a capability that is actually available to such a
group, which would not follow from a measurement on a datacentre accelerator. Its
unified memory also removes the usual constraint that model weights must fit a discrete
graphics card, letting us span 3.3 to 81 GB on one machine and ask whether the larger
end of that range is worth its cost.

One methodological choice deserves stating in advance. Guideline compliance is
substantially cross-referencing and counting — is every table cited, are figures cited
in order, is the abstract within its word limit — which are the operations short programs
perform exactly. Discussions of model capability on such tasks generally omit the
question of what a program achieves on the same items. We therefore implemented that
program and measured it as a control arm, so that what the models contribute could be
stated as a difference rather than assumed.

## Materials and methods

### Hardware and software

Measurements were made on an NVIDIA DGX Spark: a GB10 Grace Blackwell SoC (compute
capability sm_121a), a 20-core Arm CPU (10 Cortex-X925 and 10 Cortex-A725), 119 GiB of
unified CPU/GPU memory with approximately 273 GB/s of bandwidth, running Ubuntu 24.04.3
(kernel 6.14.0-1013-nvidia) with CUDA 13.0 and driver 580.95.05. Because memory is
unified there is no discrete VRAM, and the conventional GPU instrumentation reports
`Memory-Usage: Not Supported`; all memory figures here come from `free` and per-process
resident set size. Two-node measurements used a second identical machine connected by
ConnectX-7 with RoCEv2, and llama.cpp's RPC backend, with RDMA activation confirmed in
the server log rather than assumed.

Inference used ollama. Two versions were in use: 0.17.6 as a system service, and 0.32.15
installed in user space because upgrading the system service required privileges that
were unavailable. Two models released during the study required the newer runtime, so
they ran on 0.32.15 while the remaining models ran on 0.17.6. This is an uncontrolled
factor and is treated as such in the Discussion.

### Materials

The guideline document is the actual Instructions for Authors of an active journal
(4,539 words, 7,604 tokens). Using a real rule set matters: a synthetic one would allow
control of difficulty but would not reproduce the structure of such documents, which mix
numeric limits, formatting prescriptions, category-conditional exemptions, and prose
that is not a rule at all.

Five manuscripts in biophysics were written for this study. MS-A (2,832 words) carries
40 seeded guideline violations, 6 distractors, and 3 items excluded as artifacts of
writing a self-consistent manuscript. MS-B is MS-A with every violation corrected and
serves as a compliant control on which the correct output is an empty list. MS-C carries
10 internal scientific inconsistencies with MS-D as its consistent control. MS-E is
submitted under a category for which 5 rules are exempt and 8 apply.

The 40 violations in MS-A are classified as 24 EASY (decidable at a single location,
such as an abstract over its word limit) and 16 HARD (requiring two locations to be
reconciled, an ordering judgment, or noticing an absence, such as a table that is never
cited). This split separates "read a rule and apply it" from "cross-reference and
count", and the two behave differently throughout.

For peer-review support we used a real preprint and its three published public reviews,
from which we extracted 12 distinct substantive points. One is marked as consensus:
reviewer 3 identifies it as "perhaps our biggest critique".

Figure stimuli are four matplotlib-generated figures carrying five defects, plus one
compliant figure. The prompt supplies both the image and the file attributes a
submission system would report, so only three of the five defects strictly require
vision; scores on this task are an upper bound on visual capability.

Ground truth was verified before use: 31 arithmetic checks on MS-C and 21 on MS-E, 52 in
total, each recomputing a seeded defect from the manuscript's own stated numbers. This
was not a formality. A model found a genuine violation in MS-B, the compliant control —
Figure 4 was cited before Figure 3 — so the control itself was defective. It was
corrected and the affected conditions re-run.

### Tasks and coverage

Eight tasks were measured (Figure 2): guideline compliance (T1), peer-review points
(T2), reference format conversion and Japanese-language rule queries (T3), resistance to
hallucination against both the guidelines and the manuscript (T4), figure and table
checking with image input (T5), scientific self-consistency (T6), category-conditional
rules (T7), and reference verification with live calls to Crossref and the NLM Catalog
(T8). T6 and T7 were added after an early version of the suite proved saturated: a 13 GB
model reached full marks on T4 and seven of eight models scored identically on T3.

Twenty-one models from 3.3 to 81 GB were measured. Coverage is defined per task by
capability, read from the runtime's model metadata rather than assumed: 17 models on the
text tasks, the 11 carrying a vision encoder on T5, and the 15 supporting tool calls on
T8. Every model that could run a task did run it.

### Inference parameters and scoring

Temperature was 0 with seeds 42, 43 and 44; `num_ctx` was 131,072 (T2 requires this: the
prompt is 71,230 tokens, and an earlier setting of 65,536 caused silent truncation that
invalidated nine conditions); `num_predict` was 24,576 for T1. Model-default `top_p`,
`top_k` and `min_p` were left as shipped and are inert under greedy decoding. Greedy
decoding proved nearly but not exactly reproducible: seeds 42 and 43 produced identical
output for several models while seed 44 sometimes differed in length without changing
the score.

Three prompt structures were compared for T1, holding guidelines and manuscript
constant: free-form ("list all violations"), a checklist in which the guidelines are
decomposed by us into eight areas derived from the document's own section headings, and
an area-split variant issuing one call per area. The decomposition supplies structure,
not answers.

Models return JSON findings with `location`, `problem` and `rule`; only the first two
participate in matching, because `rule` quotes the journal's wording and produces
spurious matches. Each ground-truth item carries anchor phrases specific enough that a
finding about a neighbouring issue cannot be credited to it, and matching decides
independently for each item whether any finding reports it. Judging items independently
rather than assigning findings one-to-one avoids crediting the wrong item when two share
vocabulary and avoids penalising models that bundle related defects into one finding.
Keyword matching is a prefilter, not a verdict: every unmatched finding was written out
for human adjudication, because an unmatched finding may be a real violation we failed
to seed — which is how the defect in MS-B surfaced. Reported false-positive counts are
post-adjudication.

### The control arm

`deterministic_check.py` uses regular expressions and arithmetic only, reads the same
plain text given to the models, and performs no parsing of Word or LaTeX structure that
a production pipeline would have. It exists so that what the models add can be stated as
a difference rather than assumed.

### Speed and memory measurement

Prefill throughput measured during task execution is contaminated by the runtime's
prompt cache; we observed a meaningless 518,067 tokens/s this way. Speed measurements
therefore unload the model between runs and use text cut from real papers. Peak memory
is the maximum observed during execution, sampled at five context lengths from 4k to
128k to separate weight footprint from KV-cache growth.

283 conditions are reported. Ten further conditions are excluded and retained with the
reason recorded: one invalidated by the tokenization error, three superseded by the MS-B
correction, three producing no output, two truncated by an output cap set too low, and
one timeout.

## Results

### The scripted baseline reaches three quarters of the target

On the 40 seeded guideline violations (Figure 3):

| Method | Detected | False positives | Time | Memory |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Deterministic checker | **30/40** | **0** | **< 0.1 s** | **0** |
| Best model alone (`qwen3.8:27b`, checklist) | 34/40 | 0 | 524 s | 17 GB |
| **Checker + that model** | **39/40** | **0** | 524 s | 17 GB |

The best language model, using five thousand times the wall-clock time and 17 GB of
memory, adds four items to what a short program finds. The program's coverage
concentrates where models are weakest: the item that every one of 27 model conditions
missed (table numbering order), and the items missed by 23 to 25 of them (an uncited
table, an uncited figure, figures cited out of order) are all recovered
deterministically.

**No violation was detected by every model condition.** Each of the 40 was missed by
some model, which argues against a single-model design independently of which model is
chosen. Partitioning the ten items the checker cannot reach: five require semantic
judgment and belong to a language model; three are layout properties recoverable by a
document parser; one is a lookup solved by an external tool (below); one is structural.
**Five of forty items genuinely require a language model.**

### Model size does not order quality

Spearman rank correlation between weight size and normalised score, computed per task
over every model capable of running it (Figure 4):

| Task | n | *r*<sub>s</sub> | | Task | n | *r*<sub>s</sub> |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| T1 guidelines | 17 | −0.04 | | T5 figures | 11 | +0.00 |
| T2 review | 17 | −0.21 | | T6 consistency | 17 | +0.25 |
| T3 references | 17 | −0.27 | | T7 category rules | 17 | +0.21 |
| T3 Japanese | 17 | −0.20 | | T8 tool use | 15 | −0.23 |

Every coefficient lies between −0.27 and +0.25. The strongest model on T1 occupies
17 GB; nothing above 60 GB exceeds it; and within the `gpt-oss` family the 65 GB member
scores 15/40 against 23/40 for its 13 GB sibling, which no capacity argument explains.

We report rank correlation deliberately. On T5 the Pearson coefficient over the same 11
points is +0.41, and that entire apparent effect derives from one model — a 3.3 GB model
of an older generation scoring 1/5. Excluding it turns Pearson negative (−0.31), and
restricting to models of 17 GB and above gives −0.43. A single old small model suffices
to manufacture a size effect in a Pearson coefficient.

T5 deserves attention because figure checking is the task most often invoked as an
argument for capacity. Among the 11 vision-capable models, the four perfect scores are
at 17, 17, 23 and 38 GB, and the four models at 62–81 GB score 3 or 4; the largest
false-positive count on the compliant figure came from a 67 GB model. Comparing the
17–38 GB band against the ≥62 GB band gives a difference of 0.85 points with an exact
permutation *p* of 0.135. **We therefore claim no size advantage rather than a size
disadvantage**: the data are consistent with no effect and the sample is too small to
establish the reverse.

### Task rankings invert, so roles must be assigned

`gpt-oss:20b` ranks 8th of 17 on guideline compliance and 1st on peer-review points;
`qwen3.8:27b` ranks 1st on guideline compliance and scores 0/4 on tool-based reference
checking (Figure 4b). **There is no single best model.** A deployment must assign models
to roles: 17 GB for compliance, 13 GB for review support, 38 GB for figure checking and
reference lookups — approximately 76 GiB resident together, or 42 GiB if loaded in
sequence.

### Prompt structure outweighs every hardware variable

Every model measured under both free-form and checklist prompting improved under the
checklist, by 2 to 15 points. For one model the split by difficulty class locates the
gain:

| Prompt | EASY (24) | HARD (16) | Total | Wall time |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Free-form | 20 | 6 | 26 | 345 s |
| **Checklist** | 19 | **12** | **31** | **311 s** |

Structure doubles detection of the cross-referencing items at no cost in time. Effect
sizes for every lever comparable on the same metric:

| Lever | Memory cost | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Quantization q4→q8, prompt held constant | +13.8 GiB | **+1** |
| Quantization q8→bf16 | +30.4 GiB | **−2** |
| **Prompt structure** | **±0** | **+7** |
| **Model generation, prompt held constant** | **−11 GiB** | **+4** |
| **External tool for journal abbreviations** | ±0 | **0/4 → 4/4** |
| **Write the deterministic checker** | **±0** | **30/40, no model** |

The journal-abbreviation item is the clearest case: converting `PNAS` to
`Proc. Natl. Acad. Sci. U.S.A.` was failed by 8 of 15 tool-capable models without help,
because it is a lookup rather than an inference; attaching a Crossref and NLM Catalog
tool took a 13 GB model to 4/4.

Prompt structure also determines whether some models function at all. One 14 GB model
replicated a single finding 358 times in free-form, running 37 minutes to reach 4/40,
but completed in 156 seconds at 19/40 under the checklist and reached 22/40 area-split
without looping. Another model completed only under structure, and a third collapsed
under it. **A model cannot be assessed from one prompt format.**

### Memory, and which phase actually costs time

The recommended model holds a 128k-token context for **+1.1 GiB** over its 4k footprint,
reaching 17.2 GiB. KV-cache growth is architecture-dependent by more than an order of
magnitude: one 67 GB model requires +48.8 GiB over the same range, reaching 113.8 GiB,
and is the only model measured whose 128k-context footprint exceeds 96 GiB. It is also
one of three that degenerate into repetition, and the one that asserted missing
references that do not exist. **The single model that would strain a machine of this
class is the one least usable for the work.**

Which phase dominates depends on the task, and the intuition is wrong for the primary
one (Figure 5). A compliance run reads 12,272 tokens and writes 16,298, because the
model reasons at length before enumerating findings with quoted evidence: prefill takes
15.9 s and decode 501.5 s, so **decode is 97% of the wall time**. Every model that
produced a usable answer on T1 spends 95–99% of its time in decode; the conditions where
prefill dominates are the failures. Peer-review support inverts this, reading 79k tokens
and writing 3.5k.

### Whether a second machine helps depends on which phase dominates

Splitting a model across two nodes over RDMA raises prefill throughput by 1.6–1.8×,
constant across a 32-fold range of prompt length and across a fourfold range of model
size (Figure 6). The gain is compute parallelism, not memory relief: a 15.65 GiB model
under no memory pressure on a 119 GiB node still gains 1.6×. Decode changes by
1.2–1.5%, as expected, since decode is sequential and bandwidth-bound per node.

Over TCP instead of RDMA the same split runs at **0.46–0.54× of a single node** — two
nodes become slower than one, and the RDMA-to-TCP ratio is 3.8×, so the interconnect is
not incidental to this configuration.

End to end, however, the gain does not reach the workload. A compliance run of 524 s
contains 15.9 s of prefill, so a 1.7× prefill speedup saves approximately 6 s, or 1.1%.
Scaling out this platform accelerates the phase that editorial compliance checking
barely uses, and would pay only for work dominated by long inputs and short outputs.

## Discussion

### The order of work

The result with the widest application is not a number but an ordering. Three quarters
of the guideline violations are recovered by a program with no model, at no memory cost
and in a tenth of a second, and that program covers precisely the cross-referencing and
counting where models are weakest. The implied architecture places the language model
last and smallest: a deterministic layer, then external tools for anything that is a
lookup rather than an inference, then a model for the residue of semantic judgment, then
a human for what none of them reach.

**Reaching for a model before the deterministic layer exists means spending inference on
work that does not require it.** For an editorial office the first step costs programmer
time and nothing else, and it also establishes the baseline against which any later
model can be judged — which is the position we were in when we could state the models'
contribution as nine items rather than as thirty-nine.

### What the models cannot do, and what that implies for review

Certain failures are immune to capacity. An order-of-magnitude inconsistency spanning a
unit conversion — a 200 ns simulation reported as showing saturation of a process with a
2.5 ms period, a discrepancy of nearly five orders of magnitude — was missed by all 17
models. Journal abbreviation is a lookup that no size or prompt solved. These are
addressed by arithmetic checks and by tools, not by a larger model.

Peer-review support presents a more nuanced picture than we initially reported. The best
model recovered 7 of the 12 reference points in 53 s, which is useful as a check against
oversight but is not a reviewer. On the point that reviewer 3 identified as the most
important, 9 of 17 models found it and 8 did not, four of the eight having produced no
usable output at all. An earlier version of this work reported that 8 of 9 models missed
this point; that figure was accurate when the task had been run on nine models and
ceased to be so as the population grew and stronger models entered it. The claim that
survives is the aggregate one.

### Capacity, bandwidth and compute are three different limits

The measurements separate three quantities that a single memory figure conflates.
Capacity decides which models load, and the requirement measured here is 17.2 GiB for a
single-role deployment and about 76 GiB for three roles resident together — both well
inside a bench-top machine. Bandwidth decides decode speed, which is 95–99% of the
compliance task's wall time. Compute decides prefill speed, which dominates the
long-input review task and was the only quantity a second node improved. **The three do
not move together**, so a machine characterised by capacity alone is characterised for
the wrong variable: on this workload the binding limit is bandwidth, not memory.

This also bounds the generality of our speed results. The measured platform pairs strong
compute with mid-range bandwidth, which penalises dense models: a 42 GB dense model
decodes at 4.6 tokens/s where a same-generation mixture-of-experts model with 3B active
parameters reaches 43.3 [9]. On a machine with three or four times the bandwidth this
penalty shrinks, and the ranking of architectures would change even if the ranking of
capabilities did not.

### Threats to validity

The manuscripts are ours. Real submissions were unavailable, and the trade is exact
ground truth against representativeness: our seeded violations reflect our reading of
the guidelines rather than the empirical distribution of violations in submissions.
Absolute recall should not be read as expected field performance, though comparisons
between models and between prompts are unaffected since all conditions see identical
input.

The review task rests on one preprint in one subfield, and its 12 reference points were
extracted by us from review prose, which involves judgment about what constitutes a
distinct point. Most conditions are a single seed; repeats at three seeds gave identical
scores but do not establish confidence intervals, and differences of one or two points
should not be treated as separating models. We report no significance tests except the
permutation test on T5.

The figure task does not isolate vision, as described in Methods. Two ollama versions
were in use and the top-scoring model ran on the newer one, which we could not control.
All inference used one runtime family; vLLM and SGLang were not installed and
TensorRT-LLM lacks kernels for this compute capability, so throughput results are
specific to this stack. The two-node results are microbenchmarks of prefill and decode
throughput rather than the task suite executed across two nodes, and the 1.1% end-to-end
figure is computed from measured phase throughputs. Variant C of the compliance prompt
was run on 7 of 17 models because it costs eight calls per condition; no claim rests on
it alone.

### Corrections made during this study

We record these because they bear on how much weight the numbers can carry, and because
two are methodological errors likely to recur.

We evaluated one model from a single prompt format and concluded it was unusable; across
three formats it improves monotonically with structure and stops looping. **One format
is not a measurement.**

We concluded that the two-node configuration was unsuited to this workload, citing a
published figure of 37.7 tokens/s for prompt processing. When a second node became
available we measured 500 tokens/s on the same topology. The published figure was
obtained over TCP with a model too large for one node; both conditions were stated in
the source and we had not examined them. **Citing another group's number as the basis of
a conclusion requires reading their conditions.**

We reported a quantization step as worth 8 points; with the prompt variant held constant
it is worth 1. The tabulated best scores had been obtained under different prompt
variants, so a prompt effect was being attributed to a hardware variable. **Comparing
best against best is invalid when the bests come from different conditions.**

We claimed that figure checking was the task where size mattered. The claim rested on a
comparison between an old 4 GB model and a much larger recent one, and the task had been
run on only 6 of 11 eligible models — the five omitted being exactly the 23–81 GB band
that decides the question. We ran them; the ceiling is reached at 17–38 GB and no model
at 62–81 GB reaches it. **A gap that follows from capability belongs in the paper as a
fact about the population; a gap that follows from not having run the condition is a
hole, and if it sits where the contested claim lives it will be read as the reason for
the claim.**

Finally, the compliant control manuscript was not compliant, and a model found the
defect before we did.

## Conclusion

Locally hosted language models are usable for journal editorial work at a scale that
fits ordinary laboratory hardware. On a desk-side workstation, a 17 GB model detected 34
of 40 seeded guideline violations with no false positives; adding a deterministic
checker brought the pipeline to 39 of 40. Peer-review support recovered 7 of 12 points
that real reviewers raised, which is useful as a check against oversight and is not a
reviewer.

Capability did not track model size. Across eight tasks the rank correlation between
weight size and score never exceeded 0.27 in magnitude, no task required more than 38 GB
to reach its best observed score, and within one model family the 65 GB member scored
below its 13 GB sibling. What moved results instead was the framing of the task:
decomposing the guidelines into a checklist was worth seven points at no memory cost, a
newer model generation was worth four points while *reducing* memory by 11 GiB, and
attaching a bibliographic lookup tool took a failing item to full marks.

The most economical finding is that three quarters of the guideline violations are
recovered by a short program with no model at all, and that the program covers precisely
the cross-referencing and counting where the models are weakest. An editorial office
adopting this kind of support should therefore write the deterministic checks first,
attach external lookups for what is a lookup rather than an inference, and reserve the
language model for the residue of semantic judgment — five of the forty items in our
set. Whether the same ordering holds for the review task, where the deterministic layer
has little to contribute, is the question we would take up next.
